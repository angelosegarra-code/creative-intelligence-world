Two ways of understanding organization

Structure and self-organization

A highway interchange is coordinated through designed routes and rules. A forest organizes through countless changing relationships. Both are interconnected, but they work differently.

ENGINEERED STRUCTURE

Planned routes · coordinated control · predictable tasks

SELF-ORGANIZING SYSTEM

Relationships · feedback · continuous adaptation

The forest is not less complex than the highway. Its apparent simplicity emerges from relationships and feedback. Organizations need both deliberate structure and the capacity to adapt.

The central insight

The challenge is not complexity. It is how we respond.

Leadership, culture, technology, resources, and community relationships influence one another. Adding more procedures can sometimes create more complication without revealing why a system behaves as it does.

Whole Systems Design looks beyond isolated parts to the patterns connecting them. The objective is not to surrender control or assume self-organization always produces good outcomes. It is to understand where clear structures are essential—and where observation, trust, experimentation, and feedback allow better responses to emerge.

Simplicity is not the opposite of complexity. It is a different way of understanding and engaging with it.

Creative Intelligence in practice

Four perspectives on one complex challenge

Dreamer: What might be possible beyond our current assumptions?

Observer: What patterns and relationships are we overlooking?

Alchemist: What small change could we test in the real world?

Visionary: What direction is emerging from what we learn?

These are fluid modes of inquiry, not fixed personality categories. Technology may help reveal patterns or explore scenarios, but people remain responsible for interpretation, judgment, and action.

The approach

Change begins with understanding the system.

Leadership, communication, technology, culture, and strategy influence one another. Addressing one part without understanding its relationships to the others can create unexpected consequences.

Whole Systems Design begins by observing those relationships, listening to different perspectives, questioning assumptions, and identifying manageable experiments. The aim is not to impose a predetermined answer, but to help an organization learn its way forward.

A focused engagement

The Whole Systems Discovery Project

A three-week, remotely delivered assessment of one meaningful organizational challenge.

Week 01

Discovery & Observation

Leadership conversation, review of selected documents, up to three stakeholder interviews, and initial mapping of relationships and assumptions.

Week 02

Perspectives & Possibilities

A facilitated 90-minute stakeholder session to explore differing perspectives, recurring patterns, and overlooked possibilities.

Week 03

Direction & Learning

Identify two or three practical experiments, observable signals, and a 30–60-day learning roadmap. Conclude with a leadership review.
