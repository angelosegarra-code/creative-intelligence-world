What the institution gains

For the organization

A clear, limited view of enrollment and professional participation without treating private reflections as management data.

For professionals

A defined invitation and enrollment process that keeps each professional's participant caseload separate.

For participants

A choice to participate, the ability to revoke enrollment, and separate control over any private Creative Intelligence coaching work.

These are design goals illustrated by this prototype—not measured outcomes or a claim of institutional readiness.

Where the model could be piloted

Schools and adult learning

Explore learner initiative and self-directed decisions with a facilitator. Use fictional data until education-specific privacy and safeguarding requirements have been reviewed.

Advisory and professional coaching

Coordinate professionals and invitations without turning personal reflection into institutional surveillance.

Veterans and community support

Explore voluntary, non-clinical goal-setting with trained staff. No health, benefits, case-management or protected records belong in this prototype.

Experience the Creative Intelligence learning process

Fictional example only · Casey is considering a change in learning direction. This is an illustrative conversation, not a generated recommendation, assessment, diagnosis or stored record.

Casey's self-chosen question: “How can I decide what to learn next without waiting for someone else to choose for me?”

Observer · Notice what is happening

“When do I feel most curious and capable of learning on my own? What evidence do I already have?”

Dreamer · Imagine possibilities

“If I could explore without worrying about getting it right immediately, what would I want to try?”

Alchemist · Work with what is changing

“Which assumption about my abilities could I test through a small experiment?”

Visionary · Choose a direction

“What future matters enough to me that I would take one small step toward it?”

Casey's choice, not the system's decision

In this fictional example, Casey chooses to spend 20 minutes exploring a subject of personal interest and then reflect on what surprised them. Casey can choose a different action or none at all.

Professional role: Morgan asks questions, helps Casey notice patterns, and respects Casey's decision. The four perspectives are flexible modes of exploration, not fixed personality labels.

No answers are collected on this page. Institutional enrollment does not share a participant's goals, profiles or reflections with administrators or professionals.

Career transition after military service

Fictional example · Non-clinical career exploration. No personal records or answers are collected.

Alex is exploring civilian careers after military service. With a career advisor, Alex identifies a personal question: “How can I translate my experience into work that matters to me?”

Explore four perspectives

Observer · Recognize transferable strengths

Which experiences demonstrate skills I enjoy using?

Dreamer · Explore possible futures

What civilian roles would I explore if I did not limit myself to familiar job titles?

Alchemist · Test a possibility

What could I learn by arranging one informational interview?

Visionary · Choose a direction

What combination of purpose, working conditions and opportunities matters to me?

Between advisory sessions

  1. Alex chooses to research two civilian roles.
  2. Alex elects to share this goal with the advisor.
  3. The advisor asks: “Which role would you like to explore further, and why?”
  4. Alex reflects privately on what was learned.
  5. Alex chooses the next action independently.

In the illustrated workflow, the advisor sees only selected shared goals and actions; the institution sees limited enrollment counts. Alex can stop sharing. This is a demonstration of intended behavior, not a validated outcome or a live veteran service.

How the fictional journey unfolds

  1. Jordan creates the institution and invites Morgan.
  2. Morgan joins and invites Casey.
  3. Casey explicitly accepts enrollment.
  4. Jordan sees only updated participation counts; Morgan sees Casey in their own caseload.
  5. Casey revokes enrollment; the active count and Morgan's caseload update.

A responsible first evaluation

Step 1: A short discovery conversation to identify the organization's non-clinical learning challenge and required safeguards.

Step 2: One or two professionals explore this fictional walkthrough and the consent-based workflow, with no real participant records.

Step 3: Review usability feedback and security/privacy gaps before deciding whether a separately approved live pilot is appropriate.

Illustrative process only; duration, price, staffing and any live deployment remain to be agreed.

A focused pilot conversation

Rather than promise organizational transformation, begin with one professional, a small voluntary group, and a defined learning question.

Suggested question: “Can structured perspective-taking help participants identify a self-chosen next action while preserving their decision-making authority?”

Potential evaluation: participant opt-in and retention counts, voluntary qualitative feedback, and a review of whether the workflow is understandable. Any evaluation involving personal information requires a separate privacy and consent plan.

Next step: Review the fictional walkthrough together, identify the institution's intended use, and decide whether a controlled pilot is appropriate.

Prototype demonstration only. No clinical compliance certification, institutional security approval, automated invitation email or production hosting is implied.
